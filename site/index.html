II · Thesis

The case for a new primitive

Abstract

Reindustrialisation is a power story before it is a factory story. Britain is connecting tens of gigawatts of data-centre and industrial load to a grid that cannot promise to keep every connection firm — and the connections clearing the queue fastest are non-firm, interruptible by contract. That interruptibility is a recurring loss driver the market does not price. Property and business-interruption wordings were built for damage; a lawful curtailment is not damage.1,2 The electro-industrial stack needs electrons; insurers need a score for what happens when they stop.

The Non-Firm Power Risk Index is an outside-in attempt to make that exposure legible — in the Felix Stocker tradition of shipping the dataset as the product, and in the Katherine Boyle tradition of taking the physical world seriously. It scores UK carriers, Lloyd's syndicates, MGAs, brokers, assets and reinsurers on two axes, and reduces each to a single number you can rank.

The argument in brief: the peril is large and structurally unpriced; cat and cyber models cannot see it because they correlate through the wrong substrate; and the score the market needs is not hazard but preparedness minus exposure. On the provisional slice the two axes already come apart — which is why a one-dimensional ranking would mislead. What follows makes that case in full, then hands you the live index to test it.

The frame

Exposure against Preparedness

Every entity is placed on two scored axes. Exposure measures the size of the bet: how much non-firm, curtailable load sits in the book or the asset, how concentrated it is on the same grid geography, and how badly today's wordings miss the peril. Preparedness measures the ability to carry it: whether the underwriter has the data, the products, the modelling, the expertise and the capital to actually hold the risk it has written.

Margin of Safety = Preparedness − Exposure. The market deploys capacity onto whoever looks exposed — but losses concentrate where exposure has run ahead of preparedness, while the genuinely capable sit in under-priced whitespace.

Why an index

A wedge, not a data vendor

A risk score, sold as a score, is a weak business — it productises one number and waits for a buyer. That is not the point of this index. The index is a wedge: the proprietary, primary-source understanding of a peril that the rest of the market underwrites blind. The discipline that builds the index — reconciling connection registers, capacity filings, ratings and wordings down to a single comparable picture — is the same discipline that lets you actually price the gap and carry it.

The measured features are built to be exactly that edge: connection-gate status from the grid operator, the flexible-connection share from the distribution registers, aggregation over shared grid geography, and the distance between the physical-damage cover the market sells today and the parametric availability cover the risk actually needs. Whoever holds that picture first is positioned not to sell a score, but to write the cover that does not yet exist.

The discipline

No synthetic data

An AI-scored index is only worth the rigour underneath it. Every rating in NFRI carries an evidence tier — measured (a register), disclosed (a filing or rating), derived (computed from those), or assessed (sourced research judgement). Scoring is arithmetic in code, never an LLM opinion: ratings fuse a latent research judgement with a deterministic register/filing reading by credibility weighting.3 A publication gate holds the whole index at PROVISIONAL until at least 60% of the blended score rests on measured or disclosed evidence. Until the live registers and filings land, the direction is defensible and the magnitudes are honest estimates — and the banner says so.

This is the doloop discipline applied to a research index: show what is actually in force, mark what is assessed, and refuse to dress a prior up as a measurement.

The case

A peril the market was not built to see

For a century the large industrial insurance contract has answered a single question: what happens if the thing breaks? Fire, flood, machinery failure, a contractor's error — physical damage, and the income lost while it is repaired. That question built the property and business-interruption market, and it is a good question. It is also, increasingly, the wrong one for the fastest-growing concentration of insured value in Britain. The dominant risk to a data centre or a grid-scale energy asset is no longer that it breaks. It is that it is lawfully, contractually, and repeatedly switched off.4

Britain is connecting load and generation faster than the network can firmly carry it, and the system operator has chosen the only lever available at the speed required: it is changing what a connection means. The connections that clear the queue fastest are non-firm — interruptible, curtailable, flexible by contract.5,6 The operator keeps the right to turn them down when the network is constrained. For a hyperscale campus or a battery park, that retained right is not a footnote in a connection agreement; it is a recurring, quantifiable loss distribution. And it sits almost entirely outside the wordings the market has in force.

This essay makes three claims and proposes one instrument. First, that non-firm power risk is large, growing, and structurally mispriced. Second, that the models the market reaches for — the property-catastrophe model and the cyber model — cannot see it, for reasons that are architectural rather than incidental. Third, that pricing it correctly requires a different primitive: not a hazard score, but a two-sided measure of exposure against preparedness. The instrument we propose, and build here, is an index of exactly that.

The economics

Why the number is large

Start with magnitude, because it is the part the market most often waves away. The data-centre build-out alone has put a demand queue measured in the tens of gigawatts in front of the system operator,7 and the connections clearing it are disproportionately the flexible, curtailable kind. A hyperscale facility contracts for tens to hundreds of megawatts of import capacity and sells its tenants an availability service-level agreement — typically four-nines uptime. A sustained curtailment is not an inconvenience against that SLA; it is a direct breach, and power is already the single largest cause of significant data-centre outages.4

The financial value at risk in a curtailment is, to a first approximation, contracted load multiplied by hours off multiplied by the penalty or lost-margin rate per hour. None of those three terms appears in a physical-damage policy, because none of them is triggered by damage. The market is therefore carrying an exposure whose severity it does not compute and whose frequency it does not track — the textbook setup for mispricing. The premium that should attach to availability cover is a function the existing book never evaluates; the premium that does attach is zero, because the cover does not exist.

The market is not underpricing this peril. It is not pricing it at all — which is a different and more dangerous condition, because nothing in the loss ratio warns you it is there.

The mechanics

Why interruption is not damage

A non-firm connection fails in a way the market's instruments do not register. There is no broken asset, no proximate physical cause, no repair. The grid operator, acting entirely within its rights, instructs a curtailment; the facility loses availability; the penalty or the lost revenue lands. A business-interruption policy that triggers on physical damage to insured property2,1 never sees it. The peril lives in the gap between what the asset actually loses and what the contract was written to pay — and that gap is the whole subject of this index.

It is also a different statistical object from a damage book. Curtailment is a compound loss8 — a frequency of interruption events times a severity per event — and both terms move with grid constraints, weather, and the asset's position in the queue, not with the asset's physical condition. Pricing it means modelling availability, not failure. Covering it means a trigger that pays on an index of interruption rather than a loss adjuster's finding of damage, and the moment you reach for that parametric or non-damage trigger you inherit basis risk:9,10,11 the chance the index pays when the insured did not truly lose, or stays silent when it did. Basis risk is the central technical problem of the line, and the literature on how to minimise it — expectile-optimal payment schemes, multiscale indices — is the foundation this model builds on rather than reinvents.

The objection

Why the existing models don't fit

The natural reply is that the industry already has machinery for low-frequency, high-severity, correlated risk: the catastrophe model. Why not point a cat model at curtailment? Because a cat model is built around a physical hazard footprint. It decomposes a windstorm or a flood into hazard intensity, asset vulnerability, and financial loss, and its entire value is in the first module — the stochastic event set of a physical phenomenon.12 Curtailment has no such footprint. It is a contractual and operational event driven by network constraints and an operator's dispatch decision. There is no hazard module to license, because the hazard is not in the physical world; it is in the connection agreement and the constraint map.

The other instinct is to copy cyber, the last genuinely new commercial line. Cyber underwriters did learn to price a non-physical, behaviourally-driven peril — but the thing that makes cyber aggregate is shared software and shared service providers; a single cloud or a single vulnerability correlates thousands of insureds at once. Non-firm power risk aggregates through a different substrate entirely: shared grid geography. Two data centres behind the same constraint boundary are correlated whether or not they share a single line of code, and the AI-driven load that is filling the queue makes that correlation tighter, because it concentrates demand in the same few high-capacity locations at the same time.13,14 A model that measures correlation through software dependencies will score this risk as diversified when it is in fact concentrated.

So the gap is not that nobody has built the model yet. It is that the two models the market would reach for are built around the wrong correlation substrate — physical hazard in one case, software dependency in the other — and non-firm power risk correlates through neither. It correlates through the grid. Any serious attempt to price it has to start there, with the registers that describe the grid, not with an existing event set.

The proposal

The model we propose

Here is the move. The cat-model instinct is to reduce a risk to a single number — an expected loss, a hazard score — and rank the market by it. For this peril that instinct misranks the field, and predictably so. The same non-firm exposure is well-carried by an underwriter with the data, the products, the modelling and the capital to hold it, and dangerous in the hands of one without. The risk to the system is not exposure as such; it is exposure unmatched by preparedness. A one-axis score cannot express that, because it collapses the two things whose difference is the entire signal.

So we score two axes. Exposure measures the size of the bet: how much non-firm, curtailable load sits in the book or the asset, how concentrated it is on the same grid geography, and how far today's wordings miss the peril. Preparedness measures the capacity to carry it: data and monitoring, product fit, underwriting expertise, capital and reinsurance, and pricing capability. The headline number is their difference — a Margin of Safety, Preparedness minus Exposure — and it is the underwriting-quality signal the market actually needs, because it answers the right question. Not who is exposed? but who is exposed beyond their ability to carry it?

This is a deliberately conservative construct. A margin of safety does not claim to predict the loss; it claims to flag the mismatch, and to do so from outside, on public evidence, before the loss arrives. That is the standard the rest of this site is built to meet: every rating traced to a register or a filing, every assessed judgement marked as such, and nothing published as a measurement until the evidence supports it.

Four layers, one risk

The same peril sits differently at each link of the chain, and preparedness at one layer does not imply it at the next. The index scores all four.

The re-pricing

Rules that turn firmness into a fact

There is a reason this index becomes buildable now rather than five years ago. Until recently the firmness of a connection was a soft, negotiated, largely invisible attribute — impossible to score consistently across a market. Connections reform is converting it into a dated, registered fact. As the queue moves to a gated model, an asset's firmness becomes an objective register entry: which gate it sits behind, on what terms, with what curtailment rights attached.15,7 That is the moment a qualitative risk becomes a measurable one, and it is exactly the moment an index grounded in those registers stops being an estimate and starts being a measurement. The in-force rail below tracks which live rules re-price which sub-factor.

The opportunity

Whoever can measure it can carry it

The map this index draws has a quiet conclusion. The market deploys capacity onto whoever looks exposed on a damage view, while the genuine non-firm losses concentrate where exposure has outrun preparedness — and the genuinely capable sit in under-priced whitespace. The discipline that lets you see that, by reconciling connection registers, capacity filings, ratings and wordings into one comparable picture, is the same discipline that lets you price the availability cover the market has not yet written. The index is the measurement; the measurement is the edge; and the edge, not the index, is the business.

Findings

What the data shows

A model is only interesting if it sees something. The current index is a provisional slice of 55 entities across 4 layers, scored largely at the assessed tier while the live registers are loaded — so what follows is a read of structure, not a published ranking. The point is not yet the magnitudes. It is that the shape the model was built to detect is already legible in the data.

Three things stand out even on this slice. First, the field is not uniformly exposed: most entities cluster in Earning it (20) and Sidelined (14), with only 9 in the outright danger zone — Aviva, Liberty Specialty Markets, Tokio Marine Kiln, MS Amlin and 5 others. That is exactly the pattern a damage-only view would miss, because it flags as fine an entity whose exposure is invisible to its own wordings.

Second, the spread between the most and least prepared is wide and interpretable. The largest positive Margin of Safety belongs to Swiss Re Corporate Solutions (Margin +67.5), a carrier whose preparedness — rated capital, named availability capability — runs well ahead of the exposure it has taken on. The most negative belongs to Renewable Guard (Margin -27.5), an asset whose non-firm intensity sits ahead of any visible mitigation. The model is not asserting these are good or bad businesses; it is locating each on the one axis that matters for this peril — whether the bet is matched by the means to carry it.

Third, there is genuine whitespace: Beazley, AXA XL, Swiss Re Corporate Solutions, SCOR and 8 others sit in the capable-but-under-deployed quadrant. On the thesis of this index, that is where the unwritten availability cover should originate — underwriters with the capital and competence to carry the risk who are not yet earning on it.16

The headline finding is not a ranking. It is that exposure and preparedness genuinely come apart — which is the whole reason a one-axis hazard score would mislead, and a margin would not.

Reading it honestly

What would change, and what would not

The blended measured share is 32%, well below the 60% publication gate, so the index is labelled PROVISIONAL and should be read as direction, not measurement. What moves as the live data lands is the magnitude and ordering within each quadrant: as non-firm intensity is pulled from the connection registers and capital from the filings, entities will shift across the median cut-lines and the rank order will firm up. What does not change is the construct — the two axes, the margin, and the finding that they separate. The honest version of this section will be re-run automatically on every build, against whatever the registers then say.17,18

Methodology

Two axes, fused from evidence

Every entity is scored on two axes — Exposure and Preparedness — each a weighted sum of five sub-factors on a 0–4 scale, rescaled to 0–100. The scoring is arithmetic in code, never a model's free-text opinion. The judgement enters only where a register cannot reach, and even there it is fused with whatever hard data exists, weighted by how much that data deserves to be trusted.

The axis score is E = (Σ wi · reff,i / 4) × 100: a weighted average of effective sub-factor ratings, where each reff is itself a fusion of a register reading and a research reading (below). Margin of Safety is simply Preparedness − Exposure, and the two median cut-lines define the four quadrants.

Fusion

Latent × deterministic

Each sub-factor can carry two ratings: a deterministic rating derived in code from a register or filing, and a latent rating from sourced research judgement. The effective rating is a credibility-weighted blend3 — reff = clamp[0,4]( λ·rdet + (1−λ)·rlat ) — where λ is set by the evidence tier of the deterministic input. Measured registers dominate; an assessed sub-factor falls back entirely to the research prior.

Credibility weight λ by evidence tier (lowered slightly when input confidence is medium / low)
Tier of deterministic inputλEffect
Measured (register)0.95Score is the register, essentially. Research prior barely moves it.
Derived (computed from registers)0.85Mostly the computed value; small room for judgement.
Disclosed (filing / rating)0.80Filing-led, judgement at the margin.
Assessed (no measurement)0.00Falls back entirely to the sourced research rating.

λ = max(0, λ_tier + δ_confidence), with δ = 0 / −0.05 / −0.10 for high / medium / low confidence in the deterministic input.

The gate

Publication gate

No entity is published as a measurement until the blended measured + disclosed share ≥ 60% — the mean of the two axes' deterministic weight share. The gate is blended rather than per-axis on purpose: three Preparedness sub-factors (data & monitoring, underwriting expertise, pricing & modelling) are irreducibly assessed and together cap the Preparedness axis at 0.40 measurable weight, so a per-axis 60% bar is unsatisfiable there. The Exposure axis — non-firm intensity from the registers, book concentration from filings, aggregation derived from grid geography — carries the measurable load to clear the blend.

Until the live registers and filings are populated across the universe, the index sits below the gate and is labelled PROVISIONAL at the top of the page. The direction of each score is defensible; the magnitudes will move as measured data lands. The banner always states the current blended share, so the reader never mistakes an honest estimate for a measurement.
