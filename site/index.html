Abstract

Britain is connecting tens of gigawatts of data-centre and energy load to a grid that can no longer promise to keep it switched on. The connections that clear the queue fastest are non-firm — interruptible, curtailable, flexible by contract — and that interruptibility is a genuine, recurring loss driver. Yet almost no one in the insurance market prices it. Property and business-interruption wordings were written for damage; a grid operator lawfully curtailing a connection is not damage.1,2 The risk is real, it is growing, and it is being carried blind.

The Non-Firm Power Risk Index is an outside-in attempt to make that exposure legible. It scores the UK insurance-market participants standing in the path of interruptible-power risk — carriers and Lloyd's syndicates, MGAs and brokers, the underlying energy and data-centre assets, and the reinsurers behind them — on two axes, and reduces each to a single number you can rank.

The argument in brief: the peril is large and structurally unpriced; the catastrophe and cyber models the market would reach for cannot see it, because each is built around the wrong correlation substrate; and pricing it correctly needs a new primitive — not a hazard score but a Margin of Safety, preparedness minus exposure. On the current provisional slice the data already shows the two coming apart, which is the whole reason a one-axis score would mislead. The analysis that follows makes each step of that case in turn, then hands you the live index to test it.

Why now

What changed is the load. A factory drawing power on a nine-to-five shift could absorb an overnight curtailment; an AI training cluster cannot. Compute is a 24/7, always-on consumer that monetises every hour and contracts to do so — so for the first time the demand side is paying a premium for firmness itself, and the data-centre build-out has put that always-on load at the front of a grid that can no longer promise it.3,4 Non-firm risk is not an old peril rediscovered; it is a new one, created by the collision of an always-on demand curve with an interruptible grid. The market has not repriced for it. This index is, at bottom, a claim that it should — that the risk is real, measurable, and currently underpriced, and that whoever measures it first is positioned to price it.5

The parallel markets

Grid reform runs ahead of wording reform

The grid side is not waiting. A 2025 synthesis of global non-firm access identifies four dominant models — capacity-limited, time-limited, dynamic operating envelopes, and fully flexible active network management — now written into law or code from Great Britain through Spain, Germany, Belgium, and Australia.6,7 Britain's Flexible Plug and Play pilots cut connection cost by up to 87% and lead times by 57% by accepting curtailable access instead of reinforcement; Ofgem's Access SCR then standardised that trade for larger users, with curtailment limits and end-dates in the connection offer the loss adjuster can read but the BI wording cannot trigger on.6,7 The EU's 2024 electricity-market directive now requires Member States to offer flexible connections where capacity is scarce — interruptibility is becoming a register fact, not a negotiated footnote.8,6

The insurance side is moving on a parallel track, but toward capacity not trigger fit. In roughly eighteen months the London and Bermuda markets have stacked specialist facilities — Marsh's $2.7bn Nimbus programme, Aon's $3.5bn data-centre lifecycle facility, parametric SLA mirrors from Lloyd's coverholders — while S&P Global sizes a ~$10bn premium runway for hyperscale campuses and Swiss Re warns of a ~$10bn single nat-cat tail on a US data centre.9,10,11,12,13 Almost all of it still routes through all-risks physical-damage and time-element wordings; the curtailment-predictability spectrum the grid regulators now encode — fixed cap, scheduled window, forecast envelope, real-time dispatch — has no mirror in the binder.2,6

The frame

Exposure against Preparedness

Every entity is placed on two scored axes. Exposure measures the size of the bet: how much non-firm, curtailable load sits in the book or the asset, how concentrated it is on the same grid geography, and how badly today's wordings miss the peril. Preparedness measures the ability to carry it: whether the underwriter has the data, the products, the modelling, the expertise and the capital to actually hold the risk it has written.

Margin of Safety = Preparedness − Exposure. The market deploys capacity onto whoever looks exposed — but losses concentrate where exposure has run ahead of preparedness, while the genuinely capable sit in under-priced whitespace.

Why an index

A wedge, not a data vendor

A risk score, sold as a score, is a weak business — it productises one number and waits for a buyer. That is not the point of this index. The index is a wedge: the proprietary, primary-source understanding of a peril that the rest of the market underwrites blind. The discipline that builds the index — reconciling connection registers, capacity filings, ratings and wordings down to a single comparable picture — is the same discipline that lets you actually price the gap and carry it.

The measured features are built to be exactly that edge: connection-gate status from the grid operator, the flexible-connection share from the distribution registers, aggregation over shared grid geography, and the distance between the physical-damage cover the market sells today and the parametric availability cover the risk actually needs. Whoever holds that picture first is positioned not to sell a score, but to write the cover that does not yet exist.

The discipline

Where the numbers come from

An AI-scored index is only worth the rigour underneath it. Every rating in NFRI carries an evidence tier — measured (a register), disclosed (a filing or rating), derived (computed from those), or assessed (sourced research judgement) — and every rating links back to its source. Scoring is arithmetic in code, never an LLM opinion: ratings fuse a latent research judgement with a deterministic register/filing reading by credibility weighting.14 Where the live registers and filings are still being read in, the index is marked provisional, the direction holds, and the magnitudes will firm up as the data lands.

The case

A peril the market was not built to see

For a century the large industrial insurance contract has answered a single question: what happens if the thing breaks? Fire, flood, machinery failure, a contractor's error — physical damage, and the income lost while it is repaired. That question built the property and business-interruption market, and it is a good question. It is also, increasingly, the wrong one for the fastest-growing concentration of insured value in Britain. The dominant risk to a data centre or a grid-scale energy asset is no longer that it breaks. It is that it is lawfully, contractually, and repeatedly switched off.15

Britain is connecting load and generation faster than the network can firmly carry it, and the system operator has chosen the only lever available at the speed required: it is changing what a connection means. The connections that clear the queue fastest are non-firm — interruptible, curtailable, flexible by contract.6,16 The operator keeps the right to turn them down when the network is constrained. For a hyperscale campus or a battery park, that retained right is not a footnote in a connection agreement; it is a recurring, quantifiable loss distribution. And it sits almost entirely outside the wordings the market has in force.

Grid regulators are converging on a shared taxonomy faster than underwriters are converging on a shared trigger. The recent global review distinguishes four access types: a static capacity cap, a scheduled time window, a forecast dynamic operating envelope, and fully flexible dispatch under active network management with no prior notice.6 Each step down that predictability ladder is a different statistical object for pricing — fixed caps behave like deductibles, scheduled windows like off-peak clauses, envelopes like parametric indices, and real-time curtailment like an uninsured hour. Britain sits at the mature end of that spectrum: Ofgem mandated a standard curtailable option from 2023, UK Power Networks' Flexible Plug and Play pilots showed connection cost falling by up to 87% when users accepted interruptibility, and curtailment is allocated by last-in-first-out or pro-rata rules the compound-loss literature already knows how to model.7,6,17 Spain, Germany, Belgium, and Australia are codifying variants of the same trade; the EU's 2024 directive makes flexible access a Member-State obligation where capacity is scarce.8,6 The insurance market has no equivalent taxonomy in the wording — only a damage trigger that fires when none of these events count as damage.

This essay makes three claims and proposes one instrument. First, that non-firm power risk is large, growing, and structurally mispriced. Second, that the models the market reaches for — the property-catastrophe model and the cyber model — cannot see it, for reasons that are architectural rather than incidental. Third, that pricing it correctly requires a different primitive: not a hazard score, but a two-sided measure of exposure against preparedness. The instrument is an outside-in map of exactly that — who carries the bet, who can underwrite it, reconciled from the registers and filings the placement chain already walks past.

The economics

Why the number is large

Start with magnitude, because it is the part the market most often waves away. The data-centre build-out alone has put a demand queue measured in the tens of gigawatts in front of the system operator,18 and the connections clearing it are disproportionately the flexible, curtailable kind. The load driving it is the part that is new: AI and cloud compute run 24/7 and bill by the hour, so unlike the nine-to-five industrial demand of the last century they cannot quietly absorb an overnight curtailment — every hour off is a contracted hour lost.3 A hyperscale facility contracts for tens to hundreds of megawatts of import capacity and sells its tenants an availability service-level agreement — typically four-nines (99.99%) uptime, roughly 52 minutes of permitted downtime a year. A sustained curtailment is not an inconvenience against that SLA; it is a direct breach, and power is already the single largest cause of significant data-centre outages.15

The financial value at risk in a curtailment is, to a first approximation, contracted load multiplied by hours off multiplied by the penalty or lost-margin rate per hour. None of those three terms appears in a physical-damage policy, because none of them is triggered by damage. The market is therefore carrying an exposure whose severity it does not compute and whose frequency it does not track — the textbook setup for mispricing. The premium that should attach to availability cover is a function the existing book never evaluates; the premium that does attach is zero, because the cover does not exist.

The market is not underpricing this peril. It is not pricing it at all — which is a different and more dangerous condition, because nothing in the loss ratio warns you it is there.

The mechanics

Why interruption is not damage

A non-firm connection fails in a way the market's instruments do not register. There is no broken asset, no proximate physical cause, no repair. The grid operator, acting entirely within its rights, instructs a curtailment; the facility loses availability; the penalty or the lost revenue lands. A business-interruption policy that triggers on physical damage to insured property2,1 never sees it. The peril lives in the gap between what the asset actually loses and what the contract was written to pay — and that gap is the whole subject of the line.

It is also a different statistical object from a damage book. Curtailment is a compound loss17 — a frequency of interruption events times a severity per event — and both terms move with grid constraints, weather, and the asset's position in the queue, not with the asset's physical condition. Pricing it means modelling availability, not failure. Covering it means a trigger that pays on an index of interruption rather than a loss adjuster's finding of damage, and the moment you reach for that parametric or non-damage trigger you inherit basis risk:19,20,21 the chance the index pays when the insured did not truly lose, or stays silent when it did. Basis risk is the central technical problem of the line, and the expectile and multiscale literature on minimising it is the foundation serious underwriters already cite — not a novelty to be reinvented at bind.

The objection

Why the existing models don't fit

The natural reply is that the industry already has machinery for low-frequency, high-severity, correlated risk: the catastrophe model. Why not point a cat model at curtailment? Because a cat model is built around a physical hazard footprint. It decomposes a windstorm or a flood into hazard intensity, asset vulnerability, and financial loss, and its entire value is in the first module — the stochastic event set of a physical phenomenon.22 Curtailment has no such footprint. It is a contractual and operational event driven by network constraints and an operator's dispatch decision. There is no hazard module to license, because the hazard is not in the physical world; it is in the connection agreement and the constraint map.

The other instinct is to copy cyber, the last genuinely new commercial line. Cyber underwriters did learn to price a non-physical, behaviourally-driven peril — but the thing that makes cyber aggregate is shared software and shared service providers; a single cloud or a single vulnerability correlates thousands of insureds at once. Non-firm power risk aggregates through a different substrate entirely: shared grid geography. Two data centres behind the same constraint boundary are correlated whether or not they share a single line of code, and the AI-driven load that is filling the queue makes that correlation tighter, because it concentrates demand in the same few high-capacity locations at the same time.23,4 A model that measures correlation through software dependencies will score this risk as diversified when it is in fact concentrated.

So the gap is not that nobody has built the model yet. It is that the two models the market would reach for are built around the wrong correlation substrate — physical hazard in one case, software dependency in the other — and non-firm power risk correlates through neither. It correlates through the grid. Any serious attempt to price it has to start there, with the registers that describe the grid, not with an existing event set.

The incumbents

Why the energy market hasn't priced it

There is no shortage of energy-insurance capacity in London. Dozens of carriers, Lloyd's syndicates, MGAs and brokers write power, renewables and construction risk every day. So why does an unpriced peril survive inside a sophisticated market? Three structural reasons, none of them stupidity. First, the form is sticky: energy cover is sold on all-risks physical-damage-and-time-element wordings refined over decades, and a curtailment generates no damage event for a loss adjuster to find, so it never enters the claims data that would justify a rate.2 Second, the incentives point sideways: an underwriter is rewarded for growing premium on a familiar form, not for inventing an availability product whose loss history does not yet exist and whose basis risk is hard to reinsure. Third, the peril is new: the always-on compute load that makes firmness valuable is a phenomenon of the last few years, and rates lag exposure by exactly the interval it takes a loss to show up in the triangles.

The result is a market that is long the risk and short the price. Curtailment cost is already observable in the system operator's own constraint-cost data — a clean external signal of how often and how expensively the network turns load and generation down5 — yet almost none of it flows into an energy premium. That gap between an observable, rising cost and a flat rate is the definition of mispricing: the placement chain keeps renewing damage-and-time-element towers while the asset side contracts for firm power the grid cannot yet promise.4 Meanwhile the specialist market is racing to add limit, not fit: broker facilities now quote billions in aggregate data-centre capacity, carrier research sizes double-digit premium runways, and parametric SLA products exist in fragments — but the connection agreement's curtailment cap, end-date, and predictability class rarely appear in the schedule.9,10,11,13

The proposal

The model we propose

Here is the move. The cat-model instinct is to reduce a risk to a single number — an expected loss, a hazard score — and rank the market by it. For this peril that instinct misranks the field, and predictably so. The same non-firm exposure is well-carried by an underwriter with the data, the products, the modelling and the capital to hold it, and dangerous in the hands of one without. The risk to the system is not exposure as such; it is exposure unmatched by preparedness. A one-axis score cannot express that, because it collapses the two things whose difference is the entire signal.

So the market needs two axes, not one. Exposure measures the size of the bet: how much non-firm, curtailable load sits in the book or the asset, how concentrated it is on the same grid geography, and how far today's wordings miss the peril. Preparedness measures the capacity to carry it: data and monitoring, product fit, underwriting expertise, capital and reinsurance, and pricing capability. The headline number is their difference — a Margin of Safety, Preparedness minus Exposure — and it is the underwriting-quality signal the market actually needs, because it answers the right question. Not who is exposed? but who is exposed beyond their ability to carry it?

This is a deliberately conservative construct. A margin of safety does not claim to predict the loss; it flags the mismatch between the bet and the means to carry it, from outside, on public evidence, before the loss arrives. That is the underwriting standard the line needs: every judgement traceable to a register or a filing, every rating that rests on research rather than disclosure flagged as such, and no claim to measured precision until the connection registers support it.

The value chain

Four layers, one risk

The same peril sits differently at each link of the chain, and preparedness at one layer does not imply it at the next. A carrier can be capital-strong while the MGA binding its energy facility has no availability wording; a colocation asset can be heavily exposed while the reinsurer behind the cedant models only physical cat.

The re-pricing

Rules that turn firmness into a fact

There is a reason firmness becomes a pricing variable now rather than five years ago. Until recently the firmness of a connection was a soft, negotiated, largely invisible attribute — impossible to compare consistently across a market. Connections reform is converting it into a dated, registered fact. As the queue moves to a gated model, an asset's firmness becomes an objective register entry: which gate it sits behind, on what terms, with what curtailment rights attached.24,18 That is the moment a qualitative underwriting judgement becomes a field the whole market can see — and the moment live rules such as CMP434/448 and GC0166 start re-pricing the same sub-factors on every renewal.

The opportunity

Whoever can measure it can carry it

The quiet conclusion is commercial, not rhetorical. The market deploys capacity onto whoever looks exposed on a damage view, while the genuine non-firm losses concentrate where exposure has outrun preparedness — and the genuinely capable sit in under-priced whitespace. Whoever first reconciles connection registers, capacity filings, ratings and wordings into one comparable picture is not selling a score; they are positioned to structure the availability cover the market has not yet written, and to earn the premium that should already attach to it.

Where the signal concentrates

Layer 1 still contributes 9 Exposed names — carriers whose book concentration and trigger gap outrun parametric or availability product fit. That is a different failure mode from the asset case: not “curtailed tomorrow” but “writing damage-only cover on a peril whose loss is lawful switch-off.”2,1

The exposed slice

Where exposure runs ahead of preparedness

Within Exposed, the deepest negative margin is Gate Burton Energy Park — West Lindsey, Lincolnshire, England (Margin -62.6). Of 24 scored assets, 12 have a firm Gate register status and 12 remain unknown pending a Gate-2 record — the index shows the number but does not infer firmness. The honest read is that the L3 Exposed cluster (19 of 30) is directionally right while magnitudes and ordering will move as NESO and DNO registers are fully ingested.24,26

The headline finding is not a ranking. It is that exposure and preparedness genuinely come apart — across 115 entities and 130.1 points of margin spread — which is the whole reason a one-axis hazard score would mislead, and a margin would not.

Methodology

Two axes, fused from evidence

Every entity is scored on two axes — Exposure and Preparedness — each a weighted sum of five sub-factors on a 0–4 scale, rescaled to 0–100. The scoring is arithmetic in code, never a model's free-text opinion. The judgement enters only where a register cannot reach, and even there it is fused with whatever hard data exists, weighted by how much that data deserves to be trusted.

The axis score is E = (Σ wi · reff,i / 4) × 100: a weighted average of effective sub-factor ratings, where each reff is itself a fusion of a register reading and a research reading (below). Margin of Safety is simply Preparedness − Exposure, and the two median cut-lines define the four quadrants.

Fusion

Latent × deterministic

Each sub-factor can carry two ratings: a deterministic rating derived in code from a register or filing, and a latent rating from sourced research judgement. The effective rating is a credibility-weighted blend14 — reff = clamp[0,4]( λ·rdet + (1−λ)·rlat ) — where λ is set by the evidence tier of the deterministic input. Measured registers dominate; an assessed sub-factor falls back entirely to the research prior.

Computation

From register to rating

Each sub-factor's path from source to 0–4 rating is fixed in the rubric, so the same inputs always produce the same score. The table below traces that path for each one: the data is collected first, and the rating follows from it.

non_firm_intensitymeasured / derivedΣ(non-firm MW) / Σ(MW) from ECR flexible-connection flags or TEC gate status.

The three assessed Preparedness sub-factors are weight-limited and reported transparently; the Exposure axis carries most of the measurable load, since its sub-factors read straight off the registers and filings.

Evidence

How sources connect to the model

Browse by topic, search by name, or follow links between registers, products, and research anchors.

    Harness

    Method & evals

    Scoring is arithmetic in code (harness/scoring.py), never an LLM opinion. L5 is the publication gate.

    Ratings fuse latent (research) and deterministic (register/filing) inputs by credibility weighting, r_eff = clamp(λ·r_det + (1−λ)·r_lat) (contract/MODEL_SPEC.md). Quadrants use in-sample median cut-lines. Below the publication gate = PROVISIONAL. Outside-in research aid — not audited positions, not investment advice.
